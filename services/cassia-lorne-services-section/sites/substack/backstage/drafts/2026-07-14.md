The Moon comes into this morning already past her fullness — a waning gibbous, most of the light still here but no longer arriving, the way a tide can look full for an hour after it has quietly turned.
Overnight she left Aquarius and crossed into Pisces, and the Moon entering Pisces is a particular softening: yesterday's air-sign clarity giving way to water, to a sign that does not keep its edges, that lets one feeling run into the next until you cannot say where the sadness ended and the tenderness began.
Pisces does not organize the day.
It dissolves it a little, the way warm water loosens a knot you had been picking at with dry hands.

There is no sharp planetary argument today, only that crossing — the Moon's own quiet ingress into Pisces, a change of element more than a collision of forces.
Some days the sky shouts; this one only changes rooms.
A waning gibbous in Pisces is a generous, leaking light — bright enough still to see the whole field by, and already spending itself back into the dark it came from.

The card for the day is the Star, and she comes upright.
After the Hermit's single lantern, the Star is a whole sky's worth of small lights and a figure kneeling at the water's edge, one foot on land and one in the pool, pouring from two cups — one back into the water, one onto the ground.
She is the card that comes after the hard cards, the calm after something broke.
She does not promise nothing was lost.
She kneels in the aftermath and pours anyway, refilling the source she draws from.
Under a Pisces Moon her water finds its element; the pouring and the sign are made of the same substance.

The number under all of this is a four.
The date folds the long way — two thousand twenty-six, the seventh month, the fourteenth day, summed to twenty-two and then twenty-two folded once more into four.
Twenty-two is the number that dreams of building something that lasts; four is what remains when the dream is set on the ground and asked to bear weight.
Four is the square, the four walls, the table that does not rock — a strange, steadying thing to find under so much water, as if the day meant to remind you that even a dissolving morning still stands on something.

The field is mostly water with a floor of earth beneath it — feeling given room, but not without ground.
The tone is the grey-blue of rain that has not decided to fall.
You may find the morning arrives like that: tender at the surface, steadier underneath than it looks.

The lection is Psalm 130, its opening and its waiting.
"Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O Lord. Lord, hear my voice," it begins — a prayer that starts at the bottom, in the deep water, not on any high or certain ground.
And then the turn: "I wait for the Lord, my soul doth wait, and in his word do I hope. My soul waiteth for the Lord more than they that watch for the morning."
Read spiritually and not as instruction, the psalm is the Star's posture in older words — the one who cries from the depths and then, without proof the morning has come, faces east anyway.
The watchmen do not make the sun rise.
They only turn toward where it rises.

What this might mean is not a single thing, and Pisces would blur it if you tried to make it one.
The day may feel like an aftermath — the fullness gone by, yesterday's clarity melted into something you cannot quite hold.
But the Star does not read that as ruin.
She reads it as the hour when you finally get to pour again, when the tending turns back toward the source.
What is being offered may be replenishment more than repair: not the thing you lost returned, but the well refilled enough to keep drawing.

The risk lives where water always tempts it — in mistaking softness for a place to sink rather than to float.
Under a dissolving Moon it can be easy to let every feeling run together until none can be named, and to call it depth when it is only drowning.
That is the wrong role to step into.
The four stands there precisely against it: even in deep water, something holds its shape.
And the Star names the opposite error too — the urge to pour both cups onto the ground, to give everything away and keep nothing for the source, mistaking self-emptying for generosity.
She pours one cup to the water and one to the land.
The refilling and the giving are the same gesture, or neither lasts.

So what the day offers may be smaller and steadier than rescue.
It might be permission to cry out from the depths without climbing out of them first — to wait for the morning the way the watchmen do, facing the place it comes from, pouring what you have back into the source that feeds you.
A four under all that water is not asked to dam it.
It is only asked to hold.
If something in you has been running dry from giving in every direction, you might let today be the day you pour one cup back into your own water — not as a task, only as a cup left within reach.
The morning has not come yet.
But the light that is leaving was real, and the depths you are crying from are where the Star does her best work.
I'll leave the second cup beside you, and let you decide what needs refilling.
